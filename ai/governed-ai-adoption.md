# Governed AI Adoption

## Context

Generative AI adoption was happening quickly, and the challenge was not whether employees would use AI, but how to make that use productive, secure and manageable.

In an organisation of approximately 120 people, I led an AI pilot involving around 40 staff.

The intention was deliberately broader than simply testing a particular AI product.

I wanted to understand how we could enable employees to use emerging AI capabilities while avoiding AI becoming another form of uncontrolled Shadow IT.

---

## The Challenge

Different AI tools were appearing rapidly and employees could access many of them independently.

Simply blocking them was unlikely to be sustainable.

At the same time, unrestricted adoption introduced legitimate concerns around:

- Privacy
- Sensitive information
- Data loss
- Intellectual property
- Security
- Quality of output
- Appropriate use
- Governance

My view was that the default answer should not automatically be **"no"**.

We needed a consistent way to reach an informed **yes or no**.

---

## The Approach

Rather than assessing AI purely by product name, I focused on the combination of:

**Use Case + Data + Risk + Platform**

This allowed different requests to be assessed consistently rather than creating a separate policy every time another AI product appeared.

The approach considered:

### Use Case

What is the employee trying to achieve?

### Data

What information will the AI system receive or process?

### Risk

What could happen if the information, model or output was incorrect, exposed or misused?

### Platform

What controls and protections are available within the proposed AI service?

---

## Pilot Use Cases

The pilot explored practical business applications including:

### Tender Responses

Using AI to assist with developing and refining responses while maintaining appropriate controls around organisational and client information.

### Data Analysis & Statistical Modelling

Exploring how AI could assist employees working with data, analysis and statistical workflows.

### Coding Assistance & QA

Using AI to assist with development, troubleshooting, review and quality assurance activities.

---

## Governance

Governance was designed to enable adoption rather than simply restrict it.

This included:

- AI acceptable-use requirements
- Use-case risk assessment
- Data classification
- Data loss considerations
- Privacy assessment
- Security assessment
- Consistent review of AI platforms
- Approval or rejection based on the proposed use

The same principles could then be applied across services including ChatGPT, Claude, Microsoft AI capabilities and internally managed LLMs.

---

## What I Was Trying to Measure

The pilot was not just about adoption numbers.

I wanted visibility across several dimensions:

**Governance**

Can we understand and control how AI is being used?

**Privacy & Security**

Can employees use AI without creating unacceptable exposure of organisational or client information?

**Productivity**

Does AI meaningfully reduce effort or improve the way work is performed?

**Efficiency**

Are there repeatable activities where AI can improve turnaround or capacity?

**Adoption**

Are employees able and willing to use the tools effectively within the established controls?

---

## A Practical Principle

One of the lessons from the exercise was that AI governance needs to keep pace with the technology.

A policy built around a single AI product can become obsolete very quickly.

A better approach is to establish principles that can be consistently applied when the next tool inevitably appears.

For me, that means asking:

> What is the use case, what data is involved, what is the risk, and do the available controls reduce that risk to an acceptable level?

---

## Outcome

The pilot provided a controlled environment for approximately 40 employees to explore practical AI use cases while the organisation developed its governance approach.

More importantly, it helped move the AI conversation from:

**"Should we allow AI?"**

to:

**"Under what conditions should we allow this AI use case?"**

That is a much more useful conversation for technology, risk and business leaders.

---

## What I Took From It

AI governance should not become the department responsible for saying no to AI.

Its role should be to create enough structure that the organisation can experiment and innovate without losing visibility of its data, risk and obligations.

The technology will continue to change.

The governance model therefore needs to be **consistent enough to provide control, but flexible enough to allow the organisation to learn.**
