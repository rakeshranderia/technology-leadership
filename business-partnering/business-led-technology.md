# From IT-Led Priorities to Business-Led Technology

## Context

One of the changes I have increasingly focused on in technology leadership is the relationship between IT and the rest of the organisation.

Traditional IT functions often talk about "users".

I prefer to think of the people and business functions we support as **customers**.

It may seem like a small change in terminology, but it creates a very different mindset.

A user consumes technology.

A customer has a business need, an expectation of service and a voice in determining what is important.

---

## The Challenge

Technology teams naturally see the organisation through a technology lens.

Infrastructure needs upgrading.

Applications need replacing.

Security controls need improving.

Technical debt needs addressing.

All of those things may be valid, but they do not automatically represent the priorities of the business.

Without a structured relationship between technology and business teams, priorities can easily become determined by:

- The loudest request
- The most recent problem
- What IT believes is important
- Individual relationships
- Technical preferences
- Whoever escalates most effectively

This can leave technology operating as a silo even when the team believes it is being business focused.

---

## Changing the Relationship

I wanted to shift the conversation from:

**"What does IT need to do?"**

to:

**"What does the business need to achieve, and where can technology help?"**

This was the basis of establishing a Technology Business Partnering approach.

The technology team still brought its own responsibilities around security, resilience, lifecycle management and technical risk.

But business priorities increasingly came from structured conversations with the people actually running the business.

---

## Building a Cadence

An important part of the model was creating a regular cadence between technology and business functions.

Rather than waiting for projects or problems to trigger conversations, engagement became an ongoing process.

The discussions focused on:

- Current business priorities
- Operational pain points
- Upcoming change
- Process inefficiencies
- Customer experience
- Data and reporting requirements
- Automation opportunities
- Technology risks
- Future capability requirements

This gave technology much earlier visibility of where the organisation was heading.

It also gave business leaders greater visibility of technology capacity, constraints and competing priorities.

---

## Business-Led Prioritisation

One of the most important changes was making prioritisation a shared responsibility.

IT should not independently determine the relative business value of every technology request.

Instead, technology can help the organisation understand:

- Cost
- Complexity
- Dependencies
- Risk
- Security
- Delivery effort
- Technical feasibility

The business can then help determine:

- Business value
- Urgency
- Customer impact
- Operational impact
- Strategic importance

Together, those perspectives create a much better basis for deciding what should happen first.

---

## Moving Away From the IT Silo

The objective was not simply better communication.

It was to make technology planning part of normal business planning.

Instead of:

**Business → Request → IT → Delivery**

the relationship becomes:

**Business + Technology → Prioritise → Design → Deliver → Review**

That distinction matters.

Technology becomes involved earlier, while there is still an opportunity to influence the solution rather than simply being asked to implement one.

---

## Finding Opportunities

The Business Partnering model also created opportunities that may not have originated as technology requests.

A discussion about an inefficient process might reveal an automation opportunity.

A reporting problem might expose a data-quality issue.

A customer-service problem might require integration between several existing systems rather than purchasing another application.

This is where I believe technology leadership can add considerably more value than simply responding to a queue of requests.

---

## Technology Still Has a Voice

Business-led prioritisation does not mean technology becomes an order-taking function.

There will always be technology-driven requirements such as:

- Cybersecurity
- Resilience
- Regulatory obligations
- End-of-life technology
- Architecture
- Technical debt
- Infrastructure lifecycle

Part of the technology leader's role is making those requirements understandable in business terms.

The conversation becomes less:

**"We need to upgrade this platform."**

and more:

**"Here is the operational or security risk of continuing to run it, the options available, and the consequence of deferring the investment."**

That allows technology priorities to compete transparently alongside other business priorities.

---

## Outcome

The Business Partnering approach created a more structured relationship between technology and the organisation.

It shifted the emphasis from supporting users and responding to requests toward understanding customers, identifying opportunities and jointly determining priorities.

For me, the important change was cultural:

**IT stopped being the only place where technology priorities were decided.**

Technology still provided expertise and challenged decisions where appropriate, but the business had a much stronger role in determining where technology effort should be invested.

---

## What I Took From It

Calling someone a customer does not automatically create a customer-focused technology function.

The behaviour behind the word matters.

For me, business partnering means:

**Listen before proposing.**

**Understand the problem before choosing the technology.**

**Make priorities visible.**

**Let the business help determine value.**

**Let technology clearly articulate risk.**

And then make the decision together.

That is the difference between an IT function that supports the business and a technology function that is genuinely part of it.
